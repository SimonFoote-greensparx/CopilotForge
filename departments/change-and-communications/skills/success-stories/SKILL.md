# Success stories

## Purpose

People adopt new ways of working when they see colleagues like them doing it on real work. This skill finds, checks and
shares those stories so they are believed: grounded in real tasks, honest about effort and limits, supported by
evidence, cleared for data and consent, and kept current as products change. Success stories usually go wrong by
becoming marketing - an impressive number nobody can trace, a demo dressed as practice, or a story about a senior
leader that frontline staff cannot relate to - which teaches the workforce to discount everything the programme says.

## When to use

- The programme needs examples that show what good use of Copilot, an agent or a Cowork task looks like in this organisation.
- A sponsor or leadership forum wants stories to share and the programme has only anecdotes.
- Stories circulating internally contain claims ("saved ten hours a week") that nobody has checked.
- A campaign, Champion community or business unit has produced examples worth sharing more widely.
- Sceptical groups need evidence from peers rather than from the programme team.

## When not to use

- Measuring and valuing benefits formally - use `value-realisation:non-financial-benefits` or `value-realisation:financial-benefits`.
- Measuring adoption across the organisation - use `copilot-adoption:adoption-measurement`.
- Planning the overall narrative, senders and channels - use `change-and-communications:ai-communication-strategy`.
- Running Champion show-and-tell sessions and community rituals - use `champions:champion-community-design`.
- Adding a tested scenario and its prompt pattern to the catalogue - use `copilot-adoption:scenario-discovery`.

## Questions to ask first

1. What should the story help people believe or do, and for which audience?
2. Is the example on real, recurring work, and could someone in a similar role repeat it?
3. What evidence supports the claimed result, and is it measured, sampled or self-reported?
4. Have the people and teams featured consented, and is every example free of personal and confidential data?
5. Who owns the work or benefit, and will they stand behind the story?

## Required inputs

- Leads from Champions, managers, campaign reviews, the opportunity backlog and value tracking.
- Benefit and measurement records from the value-realisation skills where a quantified claim is made.
- The communications plan, so stories are placed where they help the narrative.
- Consent and data clearance arrangements agreed with HR and the DPO.

If a quantified result cannot be evidenced, either remove the number or label it plainly as the team's own estimate.
Never round up or extrapolate a single example into an organisation-wide claim.

## Decision framework

Test every candidate story against the credibility criteria before writing it.

| Criterion | Strong | Not ready |
|---|---|---|
| Real work | A recurring task in the team's actual job | A one-off demo or staged example |
| Repeatable | Others in similar roles could do the same with approved tools | Depends on special access, unusual skill or an unapproved tool |
| Verified practice | Shows how the output was checked | Output used without checking |
| Evidence | Measured or sampled result, or clearly labelled estimate | Unsourced number |
| Honest about limits | Says what did not work and what still needs a person | Only positives |
| Cleared | Consent given; no personal or confidential data; owner agrees | Any of these missing |

Choose the story type by what the audience needs.

| Story type | Use it to |
|---|---|
| Scenario story | Show one role doing one task differently, step by step |
| Team story | Show a team changing a routine together and what it took |
| Agent or Cowork story | Show a governed solution in use, with its owner and checks |
| Lessons story | Show something that did not work and what was learned |
| Peer voice | Let a sceptic explain what changed their mind, in their own words |

## Practitioner approach

Build a pipeline, not a scramble. Ask Champions, managers, campaign leads and value owners to flag candidate stories as
they arise, through a short form that asks what the task was, what changed and what evidence exists. Review candidates
monthly against the criteria. Most candidates will not be ready; some will become strong stories with a little
evidence-gathering.

Interview the team, not just the enthusiast. Spend thirty minutes with the people who do the work and ask them to show
the task: what they did before, what they do now, how they check the output, what still needs a person, what went wrong
at first and what they would tell a colleague. Capture their words. The details of checking and of early failure are
what make a story credible and useful.

Check the evidence. Where the story includes a number, ask where it comes from. A measured cycle time or a sampled
review is evidence; "it feels like I save an hour a day" is a perception and must be labelled as such. Where a benefit
is formally tracked, confirm the figure with the benefit owner through the value-realisation work. Remove any figure
that cannot be supported.

Clear it properly. Get written consent from the people featured, explain where the story will appear and how to
withdraw, and confirm the examples shown contain no personal, client or confidential data. Ask the work owner to agree
the story. Where the story concerns an agent or automation, confirm it is a released, governed solution.

Write for the audience. Keep stories short, concrete and in the team's voice: the task, the change, the check, the
result and the limit. Include the prompt pattern or steps where people could reuse them, and link to the approved
scenario in the catalogue. Choose people and roles the audience will relate to; frontline audiences need frontline
stories.

Include lessons stories. A story about an approach that did not work, and what the team did instead, often builds more
trust than another success. It shows the programme is honest and helps others avoid the same mistake.

Keep a story bank with a review date for each story. Microsoft AI experiences change quickly, and a story describing
old behaviour becomes misleading. Retire or refresh stories at their review date, and withdraw any story promptly if
consent is withdrawn.

## Outputs

- A story bank: each story with type, audience, task, change, verification step, evidence and its strength, limits, consent record, owner, channels used and review date.
- Published stories in formats suited to each channel, linked to the `scenario-catalogue` where relevant.
- A monthly candidate review note with stories approved, pending evidence or declined.
- A list of placements in the calendar section of the `ai-communications-plan`.

## Microsoft platform considerations

Stories must show approved experiences as the organisation has configured them. Check current behaviour and naming
against `references/sources.md` before publishing, and never imply availability or licensing for audiences that do not
have it. Route any licensing question raised by a story to the Microsoft licensing guide or a licensing partner.

## Adoption considerations

Stories work best when linked to an action: a scenario to try, a session to attend, a Champion to contact. Place them
alongside campaigns and reinforcement rather than in isolation.

## Knowledge considerations

Stories often reveal that a result depended on well-kept knowledge. Name it in the story and credit its owner; this
reinforces the case for knowledge upkeep.

## Security and privacy considerations

No personal, client or confidential data appears in any story, screenshot or recording. Consent is recorded, includes
the right to withdraw, and is held securely with a retention period. Data protection questions end with the DPO.

## Responsible AI considerations

Every story shows the human check and does not overstate capability. Stories about AI in decisions affecting people are
published only for solutions that have passed their responsible AI review, and say how people oversee them.

## Evaluation requirements

Not applicable as solution evaluation. Each story's claims are checked against evidence before publication, and its
currency is checked at its review date.

## Value measures

Owned by the change lead: share of published stories meeting all credibility criteria, stories by audience and role
coverage, and group-level pulse responses on whether people find examples relevant and believable. Not counts of story
views alone, and never individual measures.

## Scale notes

### Starter

A handful of stories a quarter gathered by the adoption lead and Champions, a simple list as the story bank and consent
by email.

### Scale

A monthly candidate review, a story bank with review dates, stories coordinated with campaigns and value tracking.

### Enterprise

Divisional story pipelines feeding a shared bank, common criteria and consent process, translation and regional
adaptation, and stories linked to the value dashboard.

## Hand-offs

- To `change-and-communications:ai-communication-strategy` to place stories in the calendar.
- To `copilot-adoption:scenario-discovery` when a story reveals a scenario worth cataloguing.
- To `value-realisation:benefit-owner` to confirm figures attached to tracked benefits.
- To `champions:champion-community-design` to share stories in community rituals.

## Reviewer gates

Not applicable as a formal reviewer gate, because a story does not release a solution. Stories about solutions at risk
tier 2 or above are published only after those solutions have passed their release gates.

## Failure modes

- Unverified hours-saved figures are published, challenged, and the programme loses credibility.
- Stories feature only senior leaders and enthusiasts, so most staff cannot relate to them.
- A screenshot exposes client or personal data.
- Stories describe old product behaviour and confuse people who try to repeat them.
- Only successes are told, and staff conclude the programme is not being honest.

## Sources

`references/sources.md` in this skill lists the source records behind this skill, what each is authoritative for, how volatile it is and what you may do with it. Product availability, licensing, limits and preview status change often, so check the current source before stating them, and cite what you used.

## Never

- Publish a figure that cannot be traced to evidence, or present an estimate as a measurement.
- Feature anyone without recorded consent, or keep a story live after consent is withdrawn.
- Show personal, client or confidential data in any story.
- Use stories to compare or rank individuals or teams.
